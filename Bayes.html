Convergence, LLN, CLT and Bayes

Part I explains the modes of convergence and where they appear in the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem, and why √n keeps showing up. Part II covers conditional probability, Bayes' theorem and Bayesian updating. Every simulation runs live in your browser.

Part I · Modes of convergence

§1 · The four modes of convergence

Let X₁, X₂, … and X be random variables on the same probability space.

ModeDefinitionWhat it controls
In probability →ᴾfor every ε > 0: P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) → 0the chance of a large error at a fixed n
Almost sure a.s.P( lim Xₙ = X ) = 1whole trajectories: for almost every ω the sequence settles on X
Mean square L²E[(Xₙ − X)²] → 0the average squared error (a moment condition)
In distribution →ᵈFₙ(x) → F(x) at every continuity point x of Fonly the laws; Xₙ and X need not even live on the same space

How they relate

a.s. ⇒ in probability ⇒ in distribution
L² ⇒ in probability (Markov/Chebyshev: P(|Xₙ−X|>ε) ≤ E[(Xₙ−X)²]/ε²)
in distribution to a constant ⇒ in probability

No other implication holds in general:

CounterexampleConvergesFails
"Typewriter" indicators of intervals of length 1/k sweeping across [0,1]to 0 in probability and L²not a.s.: every ω is hit infinitely often
Xₙ = n·1{U < 1/n}, U uniform on [0,1]to 0 a.s. and in probabilitynot L²: E[Xₙ²] = n → ∞
X standard normal, Xₙ = −X for all nto X in distribution (same law)not in probability: |Xₙ−X| = 2|X|

Rule of thumb: a.s. is about paths, in probability and L² are about one time n at a time, in distribution is about shape only.

§2 · Law of Large Numbers: X̄ₙ → μ

For i.i.d. X₁, X₂, … with mean μ, the sample mean X̄ₙ = (X₁+…+Xₙ)/n converges to μ. Weak LLN: in probability. Strong LLN (Kolmogorov): almost surely, requiring only E|X| < ∞. If Var(X) = σ² < ∞ there is also L² convergence, since E[(X̄ₙ−μ)²] = σ²/n, and Chebyshev gives P(|X̄ₙ−μ|>ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²).

Each line is one realisation ω: the a.s. statement says that, with probability 1, a path eventually enters the band and never leaves. The table repeats the experiment 1500 times to estimate the in-probability and L² quantities at fixed n. Try the Cauchy: it has no mean, X̄ₙ has the same Cauchy law for every n, and none of the modes holds.

§3 · Central Limit Theorem: √n(X̄ₙ − μ)/σ →ᵈ N(0,1)

The LLN says X̄ₙ collapses onto μ. The CLT zooms in: multiplying the error by √n keeps it at a nondegenerate scale, and its shape becomes normal, whatever the (finite-variance) distribution of the Xᵢ. The mode is convergence in distribution only.

The histogram shows 4000 standardised sample means; the curve is the N(0,1) density. The speed of the approximation is of order 1/√n (Berry–Esseen) and is slower for skewed distributions.

§4 · Why does √n appear?

If X₁,…,Xₙ are independent (uncorrelated is enough) with variance σ², variances add:

Var(X₁+…+Xₙ) = nσ² ⇒ sd(Sₙ) = σ√n, sd(X̄ₙ) = σ/√n, sd(√n(X̄ₙ − μ)) = σ
  • Standard deviations do not add; variances do. So the typical size of a sum of n independent terms grows like √n, not n: positive and negative fluctuations partly cancel.
  • √n is exactly the rescaling that keeps the error at a fixed scale: with a smaller factor the limit is degenerate (0), with a larger one it blows up. This is why the CLT and asymptotic inference are written with √n(θ̂ − θ).
  • In statistics it gives the standard error σ/√n: to halve the error you need four times the data.
  • Independence is what makes it work. With pairwise correlation ρ: Var(Sₙ) = nσ²[1 + (n−1)ρ]. Then sd(X̄ₙ) → σ√ρ > 0: averaging no longer removes the error (dependent data carry less information than n independent points).

σ = 1. Blue: independent data, sd(X̄ₙ) = 1/√n, vanishing. Pink: equicorrelated data, which flattens at σ√ρ.

§5 · Role of each mode in LLN, CLT and statistics

ModeWhere it appears
In probabilityWeak LLN; consistency of estimators (θ̂ₙ →ᴾ θ); continuous mapping and Slutsky arguments.
Almost sureStrong LLN: the long-run frequency of an event along the sequence of experiments converges to its probability. Basis of Monte Carlo and the frequency interpretation of probability.
L² (mean square)Proof of the weak LLN via Chebyshev; mean squared error MSE = bias² + variance → 0 as a sufficient condition for consistency.
In distributionCLT; asymptotic normality of MLE and of test statistics; approximate confidence intervals and p-values (Wald, z, χ²); Slutsky (replace σ by a consistent estimate Sₙ and keep the normal limit); delta method for g(X̄ₙ).

Summary: the LLN says where X̄ₙ goes (a number, so in probability/a.s./L² are all meaningful). The CLT says how it fluctuates around it at scale 1/√n, a statement about laws, hence only in distribution. Together they justify estimating a parameter and quantifying the uncertainty with the same data.

Part II · Conditional probability, Bayes and inference

§6 · Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem

In probability theory

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), P(B) > 0 A, B independent ⇔ P(A | B) = P(A)

It is the probability measure restricted to the world where B has occurred (more generally, conditional expectation E[X | 𝒢]). Combining it with the law of total probability gives Bayes' theorem:

P(A | B) = P(B | A)·P(A) / P(B), P(B) = Σᵢ P(B | Aᵢ)P(Aᵢ)

In statistics

Conditioning is used to learn about an unknown parameter θ from data D, treating θ as a random quantity that describes our uncertainty (Bayesian inference). P(D | θ) is the model for the data; Bayes' theorem reverses the conditioning: from P(D | θ) to P(θ | D).

Example: a diagnostic test. A condition has prevalence π; the test has sensitivity P(+ | D) and specificity P(− | healthy). What is P(D | +)?

A "95% accurate" test on a rare condition gives a mostly false-positive result: the low prior (prevalence) dominates. The curve shows P(D | +) as prevalence increases, for the sensitivity and specificity above.

§7 · Prior, likelihood and posterior

p(θ | D) = p(D | θ) · p(θ) / p(D) ∝ likelihood × prior
  • Prior p(θ): what we believe about θ before seeing the data (earlier studies, expert knowledge, or deliberate vagueness).
  • Likelihood L(θ) = p(D | θ): how well each value of θ explains the observed data, as a function of θ. It is not a probability distribution over θ.
  • Posterior p(θ | D): the updated belief. The constant p(D) = ∫ p(D | θ)p(θ)dθ only normalises.

The posterior is the prior reweighted by the data; yesterday's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior, so updating can be done sequentially. Here: k successes in n Bernoulli trials with a Beta(a, b) prior on p. This pair is conjugate: the posterior is Beta(a + k, b + n − k) in closed form.

§8 · How the prior influences the conclusions

§9 · Advantages, limitations and applications

Advantages

  • Uses prior information (previous studies, expert knowledge) and updates coherently.
  • Gives direct probability statements: P(θ ∈ interval | D), P(hypothesis | D).
  • Works with small samples and complex hierarchical models; updating is sequential.
  • Posterior predictive distributions propagate parameter uncertainty into forecasts.
  • Applications: clinical trials, spam filters, A/B testing, diagnostics, machine learning, reliability.

Limitations

  • The result depends on the prior; with little data, different reasonable priors disagree (§8).
  • Priors can be subjective or hard to elicit; "non-informative" priors are not unique (flat vs Jeffreys) and are not invariant under reparametrisation if flat.
  • Non-conjugate models need MCMC or approximations, which costs computation and requires diagnostics.
  • A wrong model gives a confidently wrong posterior.

Frequentist vs Bayesian. A frequentist confidence interval is a statement about the procedure over repeated samples (θ fixed, data random); a Bayesian credible interval is a probability statement about θ given the observed data (data fixed, θ uncertain). With a lot of data and a non-dogmatic prior the two agree closely: the likelihood overwhelms the prior (Bernstein–von Mises), another place where the CLT and √n scaling reappear.
