Riemann vs Lebesgue & the Normal CDF

Riemann, Lebesgue and Gaussian Probability: explore domain-based and range-based integration, investigate pathological functions, and compare numerical algorithms for the standard normal cumulative distribution function.

1 · Exercise 1: two ways to slice the area

Riemann: partition the domain

\[\begin{aligned}\int_a^b f\,dx&\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\xi_i)\,\Delta x,\qquad \Delta x=\frac{b-a}{N}\\[4pt] L(P)&=\sum_i m_i\Delta x\ \le\ \sum_i f(\xi_i)\Delta x\ \le\ U(P)=\sum_i M_i\Delta x\end{aligned}\]

\(m_i,M_i\) are the infimum and supremum of \(f\) on the \(i\)-th strip. \(f\) is Riemann integrable iff \(\sup_P L(P)=\inf_P U(P)\).

Lebesgue: partition the range

\[\begin{aligned}\int f\,d\mu&=\sup_{0\le s\le f}\int s\,d\mu,\qquad s=\sum_j y_j\,\mathbf 1_{E_j}\\[4pt] \int s\,d\mu&=\sum_j y_j\,\mu(E_j),\qquad E_j=\{x:\ y_j\le f(x)\lt y_{j+1}\}\end{aligned}\]

Equivalent “layer cake” form: \(\int f\,d\mu=\int_0^\infty \mu(\{f>t\})\,dt\). What matters is the size of the sets where \(f\) takes each value, not where they sit on the axis.

The pathological functions on [0, 1]

Dirichlet \(\mathbf 1_{\mathbb Q}\): every strip contains rationals and irrationals, so \(m_i=0\), \(M_i=1\): \(L=0\), \(U=1\) for every partition. Not Riemann integrable. Since \(\mathbb Q\) is countable, \(\mu(\mathbb Q\cap[0,1])=0\) and the Lebesgue integral is \(1\cdot0+0\cdot1=0\).

Thomae (\(1/q\) at \(p/q\) in lowest terms, \(0\) at irrationals): continuous at every irrational, discontinuous only on \(\mathbb Q\) (measure 0). By Lebesgue’s criterion it is Riemann integrable, with integral 0 (\(L=0\), \(U_N\to0\)). It is a counterexample to “discontinuous everywhere on a dense set means not integrable”, not to Riemann integrability.

3 · Convergence as N grows

Why Riemann fails on Dirichlet

Every interval, however small, contains rationals (\(f=1\)) and irrationals (\(f=0\)). Choosing rational tags gives the sum 1, irrational tags give 0. The lower and upper Darboux sums are \(0\) and \(1\) for every \(N\): their gap never closes, so the limit does not exist.

Why Lebesgue handles it

Only two levels occur: \(y=1\) on \(\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]\) and \(y=0\) on the irrationals. \(\mu(\mathbb Q)=0\) (countable set) and \(\mu(\text{irrationals})=1\), so \(\int f\,d\mu=1\cdot0+0\cdot1=0\). No limit process on partitions is needed.

Thomae: a useful subtlety

Each level \(1/q\) is taken at finitely many points, so \(\mu(\{f=1/q\})=0\) and the Lebesgue integral is 0. Riemann also succeeds here: \(f\) is discontinuous only on a null set, and the upper sums \(U_N\) decrease to 0.

4 · Exercise 2: why \(\Phi\) needs numerics

\[\Phi(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{z}\varphi(t)\,dt=\frac12+\int_0^z\varphi(t)\,dt,\qquad \varphi(t)=\frac{e^{-t^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\]

Non-elementary primitive (Liouville)

Theorem (Liouville). Let \(f,g\) be rational functions with \(g\) non-constant. Then \(\int f(t)\,e^{g(t)}dt\) is an elementary function if and only if there is a rational function \(R\) with

\[R'(t)+g'(t)\,R(t)=f(t)\]

(elementary = finite combinations of algebraic, exponential and logarithmic functions). Take \(f=1\), \(g=-t^2/2\), so we would need \(R'-tR=1\).

No such \(R\) exists. If \(R\) had a pole of order \(m\ge1\) at some point \(a\), then \(R'\) has a pole of order \(m+1\) while \(tR\) has order at most \(m\); the left side would have a pole, but the right side is 1. So \(R\) is a polynomial. \(R=0\) gives \(0\ne1\); if \(\deg R=d\ge0\), then \(R'-tR\) has degree \(d+1\ge1\), never the constant 1. Hence \(\int e^{-t^2/2}dt\) is not elementary, and neither is \(\Phi\).

Special function: erf

\[\operatorname{erf}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^x e^{-u^2}du=\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n\,x^{2n+1}}{n!\,(2n+1)},\qquad \Phi(z)=\frac12\Bigl[1+\operatorname{erf}\Bigl(\frac{z}{\sqrt2}\Bigr)\Bigr]\]

Giving the primitive a name makes \(\Phi\) exact as a formula, but it does not make it computable by finitely many elementary operations: every floating-point value is an approximation (quadrature, series or rational/continued-fraction formulas).

6 · Common questions

1. What is the fundamental difference between Riemann and Lebesgue integration?

Riemann slices the domain: cut \([a,b]\) into \(N\) strips and sum \(f(\xi_i)\Delta x\). It needs \(f\) to vary little on small intervals, i.e. to be essentially continuous.

Lebesgue slices the range: cut the \(y\)-axis into levels \(y_j\) and sum \(y_j\,\mu(E_j)\), where \(E_j\) is the set of \(x\) at which \(f\) is near \(y_j\). It needs only that these sets be measurable. Image: counting a pile of coins by first sorting them by value (Lebesgue) instead of adding them in the order they lie on the table (Riemann).

Consequences: more functions are integrable, and limit theorems (monotone and dominated convergence) hold under much weaker conditions.

2. Why is the Dirichlet function Riemann non-integrable but Lebesgue integrable?

Riemann: on every subinterval \(\inf f=0\) and \(\sup f=1\), so \(L(P)=0\) and \(U(P)=1\) for every partition. \(\sup L\ne\inf U\): not integrable.

Lebesgue: \(f=\mathbf 1_{\mathbb Q}\) is a simple function. \(\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]\) is countable, hence \(\mu=0\) (cover the \(k\)-th rational with an interval of length \(\varepsilon2^{-k}\)). So \(\int f\,d\mu=1\cdot\mu(\mathbb Q)+0\cdot\mu(\mathbb Q^c)=0\).

3. What does Liouville’s theorem say, and why does it apply to the normal CDF?

It characterizes which antiderivatives are elementary: \(\int f e^{g}\) (with \(f,g\) rational) is elementary iff \(R'+g'R=f\) has a rational solution \(R\).

For \(f=1\), \(g=-t^2/2\) the equation \(R'-tR=1\) has no rational solution (a pole of order \(m\) in \(R\) gives a pole of order \(m+1\) in \(R'\) that cannot cancel; a polynomial of degree \(d\) gives degree \(d+1\ge1\), never the constant 1). So \(\int e^{-t^2/2}dt\), and therefore \(\Phi\), is not elementary. We introduce \(\operatorname{erf}\) as a new special function and compute it numerically.

4. How do Simpson’s rule and Taylor series trade accuracy for cost?

Simpson: \(N+1\) density evaluations, error \(O(h^4)\) with \(h=z/N\); robust, simple, but accuracy is limited by the panel count, and it needs smoothness (the fourth derivative).

Taylor/Maclaurin of erf: converges for every \(x\) (entire function), error drops factorially once \(n>x^2\); cheap per term. But the alternating signs cause cancellation for large \(|z|\) (terms of size \(e^{x^2}\) must sum to a result below 1), and many terms are needed. The positive-term form or a continued fraction avoids this.

Rule of thumb: series near the center, continued fractions in the tails, quadrature when only the density is available.

5. Extra: is Thomae’s function a counterexample to Riemann integration?

No. Thomae’s function is discontinuous exactly on the rationals, a null set. Lebesgue’s criterion says a bounded function is Riemann integrable iff its discontinuity set has measure zero. So it is Riemann integrable with integral 0, which agrees with the Lebesgue value. Only the Dirichlet function (discontinuous everywhere) fails.
