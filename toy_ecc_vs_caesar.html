5 · Two square-root attacks: BSGS and Kangaroo

  • BSGS: write d = i·m + j with m ≈ 2b/2. Store the baby steps jG, then take giant steps Q − i·mG until one matches. Fast, but needs about 2b/2 points of memory.
  • Kangaroo: a tame kangaroo jumps from a known point, a wild one from Q, with the same jump rule. When they land on the same point, d is known. Same ~2b/2 steps, but it keeps only a few “distinguished” points.

6 · Attack cost extrapolated to 128- and 256-bit keys

The experiments on small keys (8–32 bits) give straight lines on a log–log plot: brute force grows with slope ≈ 0.96 (about 2b), while baby-step giant-step and kangaroo grow with slope ≈ 0.50 (about 2b/2). Assumptions: your PC does about 10⁷ group operations per second and a supercomputer about 10¹². Memory is my estimate: BSGS stores about 2b/2 table entries of roughly 40 bytes; brute force and kangaroo keep only a few points (about 1 KB). “Beyond any cluster” means more than about 1 PB, and “impossible” means more than about 277 bytes, roughly all data storage on Earth (an order-of-magnitude figure). Theory: 2b−1 for brute force, ≈2b/2+0.6 for BSGS, ≈2b/2+1 for kangaroo.

Reading it: the 0.5 slope is the whole story for time: halving the exponent turns 2256 into 2128, still far beyond feasible, and brute force at 256 bits (≈ 2245.8) is not a meaningful comparison. Memory is what separates the square-root attacks. BSGS is the cheapest in operations, but at 128 bits its table would hold about 264.5 points, roughly 270 bytes (over a zettabyte), and about 2134 bytes at 256 bits. Kangaroo costs a few bits more operations (2131.4 vs 2128.4 at 256 bits, against BSGS) but needs almost no memory, so in practice it is the square-root attack that matters, and it is still not feasible.

7 · Letter frequencies: Italian and Konkani

Frequency analysis works because languages are lopsided. Italian is heavily vowel-driven (E, A, I, O together are roughly 45%), and its 21-letter alphabet gives a sharper peak pattern than English.

Warning: these Konkani values are rough illustrative estimates, not a measured corpus. They vary with the text and with how vowel signs (matras) and conjuncts are counted. Measure them on your own Konkani text before relying on them. The Italian figures are standard textbook values.

Consequences for Caesar. Konkani is written in Devanagari, with about 50 base symbols plus vowel signs, so a shift would be taken modulo a larger alphabet and the key space is bigger than 26. The attack is unchanged: shifting never alters the frequency shape, only its position. Konkani’s distribution is flatter than Italian’s (its top symbol, the vowel sign ा, is only about 10%), so more ciphertext is needed to get a clear peak. Italian differs from English mainly in its vowel-heavy profile, so chi-squared with the wrong language table can pick a wrong key on short texts. Try the Italian table in section 3.

8 · Weaknesses: key space versus design

Restricted key space

  • Brute force, and BSGS or kangaroo in about 2b/2 steps.
  • A small-d table can be precomputed once for everyone.
  • Recovering d breaks all past messages to Q.

Toy design

  • Keystream is S.x repeated every 32 bytes (Vigenère-like).
  • No authentication: ciphertexts are malleable.
  • No KDF, no point validation, weak randomness for k.

Other attacks

  • Known plaintext reveals the keystream directly.
  • Side channels in double-and-add.
  • Shor’s algorithm on a quantum computer.
