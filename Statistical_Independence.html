§1 · Task and theory

Given paired observations (X, F), count how often each pair of categories occurs, compute marginal and conditional frequencies, the frequencies expected under independence, and the statistic χ². Then decide whether X and F are independent and say what the answer does not tell us.

Probability

Events A and B are independent if knowing one does not change the other:

P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B) ⇔ P(A | B) = P(A)

Two random variables are independent if this holds for every pair of values: P(X = x, F = f) = P(X = x)·P(F = f).

Statistics

With n observations, Oij is the count of the cell (xi, fj), row total Ri, column total Cj. Replacing probabilities with relative frequencies, independence predicts

Eij = Ri·Cj / n χ² = Σ (Oij − Eij)² / Eij df = (r − 1)(c − 1)

Equivalently, all conditional distributions of F given X = xi coincide with the marginal distribution of F. Under H0 (independence) χ² is approximately chi-square distributed with df degrees of freedom, which gives a p-value. The approximation is reliable when every Eij ≥ 5 (a common rule of thumb).

§7 · Interpretation

§8 · Association is not causality

A significant χ² says that the data are hard to reconcile with independence: X and F are statistically associated. It says nothing about why.

  • Direction: X may influence F, F may influence X, or neither.
  • Confounding: a third variable Z can drive both. Ice-cream sales and drownings rise together because of hot weather, not because one causes the other (try the third example).
  • Strength vs significance: with large n a tiny association becomes "significant". Cramér's V measures how strong it is, p only how surprising it would be under independence.
  • Failing to reject H0 does not prove independence: it may only mean the sample is small.
  • Causal claims need a design that controls other factors (randomised experiment) or explicit causal assumptions.
